Why this exists

Fast Fashion Creates a Clothing Problem

The fashion industry produces far more clothing than people ultimately use. Textile production has nearly doubled since 2000, while textile waste in the United States has risen dramatically.[1] Much of that clothing never returns to meaningful use.

92M Tons
An estimated 92 million tons of textile waste are generated globally each year.
85%
Is never recovered. Only about 15% of discarded textiles in the U.S. are recycled or donated; most are landfilled or incinerated.[1]
2.5–5B
Unsold garments the fast-fashion industry is estimated to have produced in 2023 alone.

The problem extends beyond waste. Clothing is also a basic expense that becomes difficult to prioritize when families are already balancing housing, food, transportation, healthcare, and childcare.

In Pulaski County, 15% of residents live below the poverty line, while another 27% fall into the ALICE category: Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed.[3] Dorcas House works directly with women navigating many of those pressures.

The research

The Design Came From the Women Themselves

Nineteen women at Dorcas House completed a survey about what they actually wanted in a blouse. The answers were specific.

84%
Wanted a slip-on design, a blouse they could pull over their head rather than a button-front style.
79%
Wanted pockets, one of the clearest preferences in the entire survey.
63%
Preferred a flowy fit, a looser silhouette over a structured or boxy one.

The remaining responses helped refine the design further. Fifty-eight percent preferred a longer, tunic-style blouse, while 63% preferred short sleeves. Color preferences were nearly evenly divided between neutrals, earth tones, and blue, green, or navy.

The survey did not point toward one imposed style. It pointed toward a blouse flexible enough to reflect different women, different bodies, and different preferences. Sizes among respondents ranged from XS through XXL, reinforcing the need for inclusive sizing.

Where it's worn

Made for More Than One Occasion

The women were also asked where they hoped to wear the blouse. Church appeared most often. Other answers included job interviews, work, birthday parties, community events, casual outings, traveling, and everyday wear. Several respondents simply answered "anywhere" or "everywhere."

The blouse needed to function as a real piece of clothing rather than a symbolic donation.

Materials

Why Hemp?

Material choice affects both comfort and longevity. Research published in Materials found that hemp-linen fabrics can provide durability along with strong air permeability and moisture management. Hemp fibers are also breathable and biodegradable.[5]

Woven treats hemp and hemp-linen as preferred materials where practical. The goal is to create clothing that lasts longer, feels comfortable, and avoids some of the waste associated with disposable fashion.

Who's behind this

My Name Is Sadie

My name is Sadie Janes.

I started paying attention to sustainability long before this project existed. I became a vegetarian in fifth grade, and that early decision made me curious about how small personal choices can create larger environmental effects.

In ninth-grade biology, I researched fast fashion for a quarter project and kept returning to the subject in later research papers and assignments. At the end of my junior year, I spent nearly a month digging further into textile waste, secondhand clothing, clothing insecurity, and sustainable materials.

I wanted the final project to move beyond another research paper. Working with the Dorcas House gave me a chance to ask women what they wanted, listen to their answers, and use that information to create something tangible.

Woven grew from that process.

Current concept

A Blouse Built From Their Answers

The survey produced a clear starting point for the final design. The current concept combines a loose or flowy silhouette with a longer tunic-style length, a slip-on construction, and pockets.

The proposed production mix includes sizes from XS through XXL. Current color planning includes white, navy, and khaki or light brown. Every major design decision traces back to information collected from the women themselves.

Built to last

Designed to Stay in the Closet

Durability matters only when clothing continues to be worn. Research on garment durability increasingly emphasizes that longevity depends on more than fabric strength. Clothing also has to fit the wearer's lifestyle, preferences, climate, and daily needs.[2]

That idea matches what emerged from the Dorcas House survey. The women wanted clothing for church, work, interviews, social events, travel, and ordinary days.

The goal is simple: make a blouse worth wearing again.
